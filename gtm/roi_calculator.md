# ShiftFlow: Manager Time Savings & Owner Upgrade Justification

This document does two different jobs, matching the two funnels in the GTM model, and it's important not to blend them:

1. **The Personal pitch** (Sections 1–2): why an individual shift manager at a convenience store, liquor store, smoke shop, bar, or restaurant would pay $9–15/month out of their own pocket. This is a personal-productivity case — time saved and fewer mistakes — not a business ROI case.
2. **The Business upgrade justification** (Sections 3–4): the labor-cost number, pulled from the app's own Labor Analysis view, that a manager uses to ask the owner to move billing to the $20–50/month business tier. This case only exists *after* a manager has real usage data — it is never the opening pitch.

Numbers below marked "estimate" or "industry-reported" are exactly that — reasonable planning assumptions, not verified facts, and should not be presented to a prospect as precise.

---

## 1. The Personal Case: Time Back

### The Problem
* Shift managers at single-location, odd-hours businesses typically report spending somewhere around 2–3 hours a week building the schedule by hand — on paper, in a group chat, or in a spreadsheet. This is a commonly cited estimate from manager interviews and comparable scheduling-tool marketing, not a measurement we've run ourselves.
* Beyond the time, hand-built schedules are hard to keep *fair*. Without a system tracking who closed last or who's owed a better shift next, the same people tend to get stuck with the worst slots — which is a common, specific complaint behind scheduling disputes and quits, even if we can't cite a precise percentage for how much of turnover it causes.

### ShiftFlow's Impact
* ShiftFlow's rotating schedule and one-click weekly rollover cut schedule-building time to an estimated 15–30 minutes a week for most single-location crews.
* **Time Reclaimed (estimate)**: roughly 2–2.5 hours/week, or **100–130 hours/year**.
* We intentionally do not convert this into a dollar "ROI multiple" against the $9–15/month price — for a self-serve personal purchase, "get two hours back every week" is the pitch on its own. Translating a manager's own time into an hourly wage and presenting it as "savings" overstates the case and isn't necessary to make it.

### Fairness, Stated Honestly
* A consistent forward rotation (e.g., open → mid → close → rest, applied in the same order to everyone) removes the manual judgment calls that create "why do they always get the good shifts" complaints.
* We do not have a verified, attributable number for how much this reduces turnover on its own — turnover has many causes. We should not claim a specific percentage reduction in quits caused by fairer scheduling; the honest claim is that it removes one common, named source of schedule disputes.

## 2. What We Deliberately Don't Pitch

* **Not legal compliance.** Most Fair Workweek and predictable-scheduling laws apply to large chains (commonly 30+ locations) — they generally do not bind the true single-location independents this product targets. Leading with compliance risk here would be misleading, so we don't.
* **Not a displaced-competitor pitch.** Most prospects in this segment are not switching from Homebase, 7shifts, or Deputy — those products haven't saturated true single-location independents. The realistic comparison is against paper, a group text, or a spreadsheet, and the pitch should read that way (faster, fairer, fewer mistakes than doing it by hand), not as a feature-for-feature competitive takeout.

## 3. The Business Case: What Feeds the Owner Upgrade

This section is not a pitch to run cold. It is the calculation a manager's own Labor Analysis data performs *after* weeks of real usage, which then becomes the one number used in the owner-upgrade conversation (`sales_pitch_deck.md`).

### Unintended Overtime
* Hand-built schedules at extended-hours, short-staffed locations commonly produce some amount of unplanned overtime when a manager has to patch a last-minute gap with whoever's available, regardless of hours already worked that week.
* ShiftFlow's Labor Analysis view surfaces overtime risk before a schedule is published, which lets a manager fix it before it becomes a paycheck, rather than after.
* **The number that matters for the upgrade pitch is whatever Labor Analysis actually shows for that specific location over the weeks it's been in use** — not a generic industry assumption. A manager should pull this directly from their own account, not from a benchmark in this document.

### Manager Time, Converted to a Business Argument (Only for This Section)
* Unlike Section 1, the business-upgrade case can fairly convert manager time into a dollar figure, because here we're asking the *owner* to value their manager's time, not asking the manager to value their own.
* If a manager's fully loaded time is worth roughly $X/hour and ShiftFlow reclaims roughly 2–2.5 hours/week, that's a rough $(2–2.5 × 52 × X) range per year in managerial time — a planning estimate the manager can fill in with their own numbers, not a figure we should pre-populate with an assumed wage.

### Why This Matters More Than the Dollar Figure: Manager Turnover
* Industry-reported estimates put shift-manager turnover in retail and food service at roughly **28–47% per year**, varying by segment — again, an estimate, not a figure we've verified firsthand.
* The business implication isn't really about dollars: a **Personal**-tier account is tied to one manager's card and login. When that manager leaves — which, at this turnover rate, is a "when," not an "if," for most locations within a couple of years — the subscription, the schedule history, and the setup leave with them.
* A **Business**-tier account, paid by and belonging to the location, survives the manager's departure. That continuity — not a bigger feature set — is the core argument for the owner to take over billing, and it should be stated to the owner in those terms rather than oversold as a compliance or ROI multiple.

---

## 4. What This Document Is Not

* This is not a guaranteed-savings claim to put in marketing copy. Every number above is a planning estimate or an industry-reported range, and should be labeled as such wherever it's reused.
* We do not compute or claim a single blended "ROI multiple" (e.g., "13x ROI") for either tier. For Personal, the pitch is time and fairness, not a payback calculation. For Business, the number that matters is the specific location's own Labor Analysis data, not a modeled average.
